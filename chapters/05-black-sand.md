# Chapter 5: Black Sand

The old sail lofts crouched at the eastern end of the harbour, three long buildings with tarred boards, patched slate, and warning notices fixed to every door. In summer, visitors photographed them as picturesque decay. In winter, with rain coming sideways across the yard, they looked like what they were: rot with a view.

Theo Sparke had printed them differently. His planning boards showed warm brick, brass lamps, balconies, potted olives, and a woman in white linen holding coffee where generations of men had once cursed into their sleeves while mending canvas. SPARKE HARBOUR QUARTER, the largest board read. HISTORY, REIMAGINED.

Someone had written in black marker beneath it: HISTORY, EVICTED.

Kit studied the graffiti. "Concise."

"Pelham Hard has always had literary ambition."

The strip of beach behind the lofts was not like the shingle under the promenade. It was narrow, dark, and gritty, the remains of an old kiln and centuries of boat repair. The sand clung to soles in black crescents. It had clung to Adrian Rusk's cuff as well.

Rowan knelt near the slip. The tide had scrubbed most of the surface clean, but under the lee of a broken wall the grit still held marks: the double track of something wheeled, the crescent bite of a boat keel, and a partial boot print too smeared for size.

"Could launch a dinghy here," Kit said.

"People do. Quietly."

"Your tone implies personal history."

"My tone implies jurisdiction."

He smiled and took photographs.

The door to the nearest loft opened behind them. Theo Sparke stepped out wearing a waxed jacket too new to have earned its creases. He was fortyish, clean-shaven, good-looking in the manner of men who had been told so early and had never questioned the maintenance costs. His boots were muddy. His smile arrived before his welcome.

"Inspector Bell. Sergeant Shaw. I wondered when you would come to admire the future."

"We're more interested in last night," Rowan said.

"Aren't we all? Poor Adrian. Appalling business."

"You argued with him."

"Adrian argued at me. There is a difference."

"About the Mercy Gale."

Theo's smile thinned. "He had developed a theory. Retired men do. Some build model railways. Adrian accused dead philanthropists."

"Your father."

"My father, yes. Although dead philanthropist has a certain grandeur."

Kit said, "Where were you after you left the pub?"

"I gave you my calls."

"Calls can be made from many places."

"And murders can be imagined by policemen trying to look useful, but we must all resist temptation."

Rowan looked at the planning board. "Adrian's objection could delay your scheme."

"Everyone's objection can delay this scheme. That is the national talent. One man with a printer and a grudge can hold up forty jobs, twelve homes, six apprenticeships, and a sea wall Pelham Hard desperately needs."

"Luxury flats and a boutique hotel."

"With a sea wall."

"And Cormorant House."

Theo glanced towards the island. "Stabilised, restored, interpreted for public benefit."

"Sold."

"Maintained," he said. "Ruins are romantic until someone has to pay for scaffolding."

Before Rowan could answer, a dark blue Volvo turned into the yard. It stopped cleanly beside the planning boards. Eleanor Sparke got out with one gloved hand on the door frame and a silver-topped walking stick in the other.

She was smaller than Rowan expected. Age had refined rather than reduced her: white hair pinned low, navy coat, pearl earrings, a face composed around the assumption that others would wait until she finished speaking. Her stick touched the ground lightly. It did not seem necessary for balance. It seemed ceremonial, like a sceptre made acceptable by orthopaedics.

"Theo," she said. "You are keeping these officers in the rain."

"They chose it."

"Then we must admire their dedication." She turned to Rowan. "Inspector Bell. Your father was kind to me once. I have not forgotten it."

It was a neat opening, intimate enough to unsettle, public enough to deny purpose.

"I'll tell him," Rowan said.

Eleanor's gaze rested on her face. "Please do. How is Max?"

"Not part of this inquiry."

"Of course." A pause, almost a bow. "How may we help?"

"Did Adrian Rusk contact you last week?"

"Adrian contacted everyone. He had mistaken persistence for charm."

"About the harbour log?"

Theo looked sharply at his mother. Eleanor did not look at him.

"He claimed to have found a document," she said. "He also claimed my late husband was a coward, a criminal, and, if I recall correctly, a parasite in a blazer. Adrian favoured excess."

"Did you meet him?"

"I declined to."

"Did you text him yesterday?"

"I do not have his number."

Kit made a note.

Eleanor saw it. "That means you will now prove I did, which is fair. Please do."

Rowan looked at the stick. Its handle was shaped like a cormorant's head, silver worn smooth where fingers held it. A dark wool glove covered Eleanor's right hand. The wool was fine, almost black.

"Where were you between eight and ten last night?"

"At home. Alone until Theo came in shortly after ten."

"Can anyone confirm that?"

"My house has alarms, cameras, and a cleaner who believes dust is a moral failure. I imagine one of those may assist."

Theo shifted. It was small, but Rowan caught it.

"Problem?" she asked.

"No," he said.

"Then stand still."

For the first time, Eleanor Sparke smiled.

Back at the borrowed incident room, the pathologist's update waited in Rowan's inbox. The bruise on Adrian's temple was consistent with a rounded metal edge. Not a sharp fall against stone. A deliberate blow could not be ruled out. The fibres in the wound were dark wool, fine grade. There was a faint residue on the coat collar: beeswax and silver polish.

Kit read over her shoulder. "A polished metal object with wool nearby. That narrows it to every elderly person in Sussex in winter."

"Don't be ageist. It also includes half the middle-aged."

"You're thinking about Eleanor's stick."

"I'm thinking about evidence."

"Of course."

Rowan opened the scene photographs. Adrian's right pocket, turned out. Missing notebook. Missing log page. Head injury. False tide time. Black grit.

The killer had known three things: that Adrian had proof, that he could be lured with the promise of more, and that the tide would finish what a blow began.

Outside the council-room windows, gulls dipped and rose in the wind over Pelham Hard. Rowan watched them for a moment. Her father used to say gulls never wasted effort. They let the weather do the work.

So had Adrian's killer.
