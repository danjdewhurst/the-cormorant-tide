# Chapter 9: The False Light

Eleanor Sparke received the warrant at half past eight in the morning wearing pearls, lipstick, and an expression of courteous injury.

Sparke House stood above the town behind flint walls and a garden clipped into obedience. From its terrace, Pelham Hard arranged itself beautifully below: harbour, church, sail lofts, causeway, island. It was the view printed on postcards and planning brochures, the view that made neglect look like potential.

"Inspector," Eleanor said, reading the warrant without haste. "You might have telephoned."

"Then it would have been a request."

"And this is theatre."

"This is a search."

Theo appeared behind her in a shirt buttoned wrong at the throat. "Mother?"

"Go and make coffee, darling. The police have mistaken decisiveness for evidence."

Rowan watched him obey. It was not fear exactly. It was training.

They searched the study first. It was a handsome room full of maritime paintings, leather chairs, and silver-framed photographs from charity dinners. Christopher Sparke looked out from several of them, sometimes in black tie, sometimes in sailing waterproofs, always lit by admiration.

Kit found the first useful item in the lower drawer of Eleanor's desk: a tide-board key on a brass ring, untagged, wrapped in a folded programme from a lifeboat memorial service.

"Not official," he said.

Eleanor, standing at the doorway with her solicitor on speakerphone, did not blink. "I have chaired public safety committees for thirty years. Keys accumulate."

"So do questions," Rowan said.

In the boot room they found a pair of navy rubber-soled boots with black grit in the tread. Eleanor said the grit came from inspecting the sail lofts with Theo. That was possible. On a hook beside the coats hung dark wool gloves. Eleanor said she owned many gloves. That was true.

In the hall stand was the silver-topped walking stick.

The cormorant head gleamed under the lights. One eye was mother-of-pearl. The other had been replaced with a bead of plain black glass, glued crudely into the socket.

Rowan did not touch it. "When was that repaired?"

"Recently," Eleanor said.

"By whom?"

"My hands are still steady enough for glue."

"Why not mention it when we found a mother-of-pearl fragment on the island?"

"Because I was not aware your fragment was applying for membership of my belongings."

Kit photographed the stick in place before bagging it. The solicitor's voice crackled from the phone, urging restraint in a tone that suggested both cost and exhaustion.

The alarm logs did more damage than the gloves. Sparke House had been unset at 20:04 on the night Adrian died and reset at 22:17. Eleanor said Theo must have done it. Theo said nothing until Rowan asked him in the kitchen, away from his mother.

"She was out," he said at last.

"Where?"

"I don't know."

"You told us she was home."

"I thought she had met Adrian. To pay him off, or frighten him, or do whatever people do when they have more nerve than judgment." He pressed both hands to his face. "I didn't think she had killed him."

"Why would she pay him off?"

Theo looked towards the study. "Because he had a page. Because if the Mercy Gale story fell apart, so did half the moral scaffolding this town has built around us. The development would be dead. The charitable trust would be investigated. Mother's reputation would be shredded."

"And yours?"

He laughed once, bitterly. "Mine is mostly laminate."

Rowan believed him more in that moment than she had when he smiled.

By noon, forensics had Eleanor's prints. By three, they had a match to the partial print on Lily's cracked head torch. Not complete, not pretty, but enough to place Eleanor's hand on the torch after Adrian's blood was on it.

At four, Kit came in with phone data.

"The burner that texted Adrian was purchased in Eastbourne eight days ago," he said. "Cash. No CCTV usable. It pinged off the Pelham mast twice on the murder night, then died. But one number called it that afternoon from a withheld landline."

"Sparke House?"

"No. Heritage Trust office."

Rowan closed her eyes for a second. Eleanor had keys to rooms that had not been built yet.

"Anything else?"

"Nessa found something. She says Adrian left a note in the archive margin. Not a note note. Adrian being Adrian."

The note was on a photocopy of an eighteenth-century island plan, written in Adrian's tight hand:

False light kept in bird house. Ask E.S. why she saved the knife.

Nessa stood beside the table, arms folded. "Bird house means Cormorant House. He was pleased with himself when he said it."

"False light?" Kit asked.

"In wrecking lore, false lights lured ships onto rocks," Nessa said. "Adrian used the phrase for any official lie. He had themes. Unfortunately."

Rowan looked at the island plan. A small outbuilding was marked beside the ruined boathouse, no longer standing.

"Could there be another hiding place?"

"There are voids everywhere," Nessa said. "The place is falling apart."

"If Eleanor killed Adrian for what he found, why leave anything there?"

"Because the tide was coming in," Kit said. "Because moving a body took longer than she expected. Because even murderers have bad time management."

Rowan thought of the false tide board, the altered number giving Adrian an illusion of time. Perhaps Eleanor had believed her own adjustment too. Perhaps she had not realised how little margin the island allowed.

They could apply for another search, flood the island with officers, and find everything or nothing. But if Eleanor believed Adrian had hidden proof in Cormorant House, and if she had seen the warrant, the missing eye, the key taken from her desk, she might do what frightened people did.

She might return to the one place she thought still contained her life.

Rowan looked at Kit.

"Low water tonight?" he asked.

"Twenty-three eighteen."

"I'll bring coffee."

"Bring Mara."

"You trust her?"

Rowan folded the photocopy and placed it in an evidence sleeve. "On a causeway at night, I trust the harbourmaster more than the table."
