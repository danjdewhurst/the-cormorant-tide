# Chapter 6: The Girl in the Boathouse

They found Lily Tye in the old lifeboat boathouse, which was no longer used for lifeboats and not quite used for anything else. It held stacked chairs for summer fetes, retired oars, three broken display boards, and a smell of dust over salt.

She had wedged herself behind a clinker-built dinghy with peeling blue paint and was eating crisps from a family bag with the grim concentration of the frightened.

"I'm not running," she said before Rowan spoke.

"You're hiding."

"Different."

"More honest, at least."

Lily glanced at Kit. "Is he nice?"

"No," Kit said.

She blinked.

"But I'm fair," he added.

That earned him the smallest possible smile.

Rowan sat on an upturned crate, leaving space between them. "Nessa says you found the tin under the sail lofts."

"I didn't steal anything. It was in the mud."

"I didn't say you stole it."

"Everyone says mudlarking is stealing when poor people do it and archaeology when people with degrees do it."

Kit looked down at his notebook, hiding amusement.

"Did Adrian know where you found it?" Rowan asked.

Lily picked at the crisp packet seam. "He asked. I didn't tell him at first. He got all excited, which was weird because it was mostly pipe bits and buttons. Then he said the paper mattered. Said it could clear a dead boy's name."

"Eddie Lanyon."

"Yeah. My mum knew his sister. Everyone did, apparently. This town is basically one big group chat with gravestones."

"Did Adrian take a page?"

Lily nodded. "Nessa said no, it needed preserving, but he said the tide wouldn't wait."

"What tide?"

"The cormorant tide." She looked embarrassed. "That's what he called it. The very low spring tide when you can get into the cellar at the island house. He said the birds knew because they moved down to the lower rocks. He made everything sound like a riddle even when he was asking for a stapler."

Rowan felt the investigation tilt. "He went to St Osyth's Eye before he died?"

"Earlier. Afternoon. He borrowed my head torch."

"Did he return it?"

"No."

"Did he say what he found?"

Lily shook her head, then stopped. "He said, 'There it is, the thing they couldn't drown.'"

"To you?"

"To himself. He did that."

Kit asked, "Why did you run from the museum?"

Lily's hand closed around the crisp packet until it crackled. "Because someone was in the archive last night."

Rowan waited.

"After Adrian died, I mean. I couldn't sleep, so I went to check the tin was still there. Stupid. The side door sticks if you know where to kick it. Someone was upstairs already. I heard boxes moving. Then I smelled this perfume."

"What kind?"

"Old-lady flowers. Sorry."

"Violets?" Rowan asked.

Lily looked at her. "Maybe. Like Parma Violets but expensive."

Eleanor Sparke had smelled faintly of violets in the rain.

"Did you see who it was?"

"No. I hid in the loo until they left."

"Did you tell Nessa?"

"She'd have killed me for breaking in."

"Lily."

"Fine. She'd have been disappointed, which is worse."

Kit said, "Was anything missing?"

"Adrian's photocopy folder was open. The Mercy Gale one. I think they took a photograph, maybe. I heard the phone click. Then I got scared and flushed the loo so they'd think someone was meant to be there."

"Good thinking," Rowan said.

Lily's face flickered, not quite ready to accept praise.

"Did anyone threaten you?"

She looked away.

"Lily."

From her parka pocket she took a folded sheet of paper and handed it over. It was cheap printer paper, damp at one corner. The words had been typed in a large plain font:

MUD KEEPS WHAT IT IS GIVEN. SO SHOULD YOU.

No signature.

"It was in my bike basket this morning," she said. "Mum thinks it's from some boy. I let her."

Rowan folded the paper into an evidence sleeve. "You're not going back to school today. Kit will take you and your mum to the station. We'll get a formal statement."

"Do I have to?"

"Yes."

"Will Nessa be angry?"

"Almost certainly."

Lily sighed. "Fine."

Outside, the boathouse doors banged in the wind. Rowan stepped into the yard first. Across the quay, Eleanor Sparke's Volvo moved slowly past the museum and up towards the High Street. It did not stop. It did not need to.

Kit came out with Lily a few paces behind him.

"Parma Violets but expensive," he said quietly.

"Put it on the board exactly like that."

"With quote marks?"

"With pride."

At the station, Lily's mother arrived in a supermarket fleece and a state of controlled terror. Nessa arrived ten minutes later and did, as predicted, become disappointed in a way that made Lily cry faster than anger would have done.

Rowan left them with Kit and returned to the archive with a warrant application already forming in her mind. The Mercy Gale folder was still open on Nessa's table. One document had been moved from its sleeve and replaced the wrong way round.

It was a copy of a 1998 invoice from Sparke Marine Services for repairs to a private launch named Osprey. The date was three days after the Mercy Gale. The listed work was bland: hull patch, stern rail, repainting. But the amount was not bland, and neither was the handwritten note at the bottom.

Urgent. No insurance. No report.

The initials beside it were E.S.

Rowan photographed the page, then stood in the quiet archive listening to the old building creak. The town had spent decades telling itself the sea had taken two men. But paper had survived in the mud, and paper, unlike people, did not learn to be grateful for silence.
