# Chapter 12: After the Tide

Adrian Rusk's funeral took place on a wet Tuesday, which would have pleased nobody and suited him perfectly.

The church was fuller than Rowan expected. Not because Adrian had been loved. He had not made that easy. But he had been present in the town's life with such irritating force that his absence required attendance. Nessa Quill sat near the front, jaw set. Lily Tye sat beside her mother and cried with the frankness of someone young enough not to ration feeling. Mara stood at the back in harbour uniform. Theo Sparke came late and left early. No one stopped him.

Eleanor did not attend.

The vicar spoke carefully about truth, scholarship, and the courage to trouble settled waters. It was not a phrase Adrian would have tolerated, but the effort was decent. Outside, under umbrellas, people said the usual things in low voices. Shocking. Terrible. Who would have thought. Underneath, the harder sentences moved: I knew there was something wrong. My dad always said. Poor Eddie. Poor Jack. Poor everyone, once the lie had finished with them.

The council postponed the marina vote. Then, under pressure from the reopened Mercy Gale inquiry, the heritage trust withdrew its support. Theo Sparke issued a statement about reflection, consultation, and respect for community feeling. Kit read it aloud in the incident room with the flat brilliance of a man narrating hostage text.

Nessa began the work of rebuilding the museum display. She removed Christopher Sparke's central portrait herself. Lily helped carry the old board upstairs.

"We're not making a revenge display," Nessa told Rowan when she visited. "Just an accurate one."

"That may feel like revenge to some people."

"Good."

On the new temporary board, Eddie Lanyon's photograph stood beside Jack Prowse's and the other crew. Under it, in Nessa's precise lettering, was a single line:

The 1998 account is under formal review.

It was not justice. It was a door opened after too many years of being painted shut.

Rowan went to see Max on the Friday.

He was in the garden, pruning roses badly. Her mother had grown them with skill and a tolerance for thorns that Rowan had not inherited. Max cut one stem too low, swore, and looked up as if the bush had betrayed him.

"You'll kill that," Rowan said.

"Everything dies. Some things need encouragement."

She took the secateurs from him before horticulture became manslaughter.

Inside, she made tea. He had the local paper folded on the table. ELEANOR SPARKE CHARGED. MERCY GALE CASE REOPENED. Beneath it, a smaller headline announced storm damage to the east groyne, because life enjoyed scale more than people did.

"They'll want to speak to you again," Rowan said.

"I know."

"Proper statement this time."

"I know that too."

He sounded old. Not evasive, not defensive. Old.

Rowan sat opposite him. "You told Eleanor to let the dead rest."

He closed his eyes briefly. "I did."

"Why?"

"Because I was tired. Because I thought rest was all we had left to give them. Because I was wrong."

The answer did not heal anything. It did not need to. Rowan had solved enough cases to distrust sudden repair. People wanted confessions to clean rooms they had spent decades dirtying. Usually a confession only opened the window.

"Eddie's sister is coming down next week," she said. "Nessa found her."

Max nodded.

"You should speak to her."

"Will you come with me?"

Rowan looked at her father: brave enough once to go out in a storm, not brave enough afterwards to stand in a room. Both facts true. Both his.

"Yes," she said.

That evening, Rowan walked down to the harbour alone. The tide was low, the causeway open, the island dark against a washed orange sky. The corrected board showed the times in clean black numbers. A uniformed constable had been posted near it for the week, partly for safety and partly because symbols mattered when facts had been misused.

Mara was on the quay, checking mooring lines.

"You're off duty," she said.

"You're not."

"Harbour isn't fond of shifts."

They stood together without saying anything. Across the water, St Osyth's Eye held its ruined house and its roosting birds. The cormorants gathered along the roofline, ungainly and exact. One opened its wings to the last light.

"I should have told you about Dad sooner," Mara said.

"Yes."

"You could pretend to be polite."

"I've had a long week."

Mara accepted that. "He'll give a statement. Patrick. He says he saw Eleanor's boat coming back from the kiln beach. He thought if he kept out of it, no one would ask him about 1998."

"And now?"

"Now everyone is asking about 1998."

"Good."

Mara looked sideways at her. "Are you staying?"

The question meant in town, in the job, in reach of the past. Rowan watched water move through the harbour mouth, darkening as evening came on. For years she had thought leaving Pelham Hard made her honest about it. Distance had allowed her to dislike the place cleanly. Coming back had made everything less clean and more useful.

"For now," she said.

Mara nodded as if that were an answer the tide might respect.

At the warning board, Lily Tye was showing two tourists how to read the safe crossing times. She spoke with grave authority, pointing from the printed table to the water and back again. The tourists listened. People did, sometimes, when the person speaking had earned the right to be believed.

The cormorants lifted from the island one by one.

They flew low over the causeway, black against the fading sky, and for a moment their shadows passed over the stones where Adrian Rusk had died. Then they were gone towards open water, leaving the town with the tide, the truth, and the work of learning what to do with both.
