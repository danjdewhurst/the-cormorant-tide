# Chapter 1: The Body at St Osyth's Eye

The sea gave Adrian Rusk back at dawn.

It did so without ceremony, withdrawing from the causeway stone by stone until the first constable on scene could see the black shape lying between the third and fourth marker posts. Above him the cormorants stood on the ruined roof of St Osyth's Eye, wings opened like undertakers' coats, drying themselves in the cold light.

Detective Inspector Rowan Bell arrived twenty minutes later with sand already in her shoes and the taste of bad coffee at the back of her throat. She had driven from Lewes through lanes silvered with rain, past fields where sheep stood stunned and white against the morning. Pelham Hard appeared at the last bend, harbour masts pricking the sky, gulls ragging one another over the fish quay, the chalk headland turning pink as if the day meant to be pretty in spite of itself.

She had not worked a death here before. She had worked burglaries, missing teenagers, assaults outside pubs, and one deeply stupid dispute about lobster pots that had produced a knife but no lasting injury. A body on the causeway was different. The town would feel it underfoot.

DS Kit Shaw was waiting by the cordon in a navy waterproof, hair flattened to his skull.

"Local historian," he said. "Dr Adrian Rusk. Sixty-four. Found by a dog walker at six ten, once the tide started to clear the stones."

"Dog walker crossed the cordon?"

"Dog crossed the cordon. Owner followed. Dog disappointed by our response."

Rowan looked past him. The causeway ran from the shingle beach to the island in a shallow curve, a spine of old stone usually hidden by water. Warning boards stood at either end. The nearer one had the usual red letters: TIDES RISE QUICKLY. DO NOT CROSS OUTSIDE SAFE TIMES. Beneath it, screwed into a glass-fronted case, the harbour tide board listed yesterday's low water in black plastic numbers.

Low water: 21:40.

Rowan knew enough, without opening an app, to distrust it. She had grown up with the tide as a second clock. The evening spring ebb had been earlier than that. Much earlier.

"Where's the harbourmaster?"

Kit pointed to a woman standing beyond the ambulance crew, one hand on the rail, her face turned towards the island. Mara Fenwick had not changed as much as Rowan had expected. The same broad shoulders, the same dark hair cut at her jaw, the same habit of looking at weather as if it were an argument she intended to win.

Mara saw Rowan and did not smile.

"Bell," she said.

"Fenwick."

For a moment they were fifteen again on the harbour wall, both pretending not to be afraid of jumping. Then the moment shut.

"You knew him?" Rowan asked.

"Everyone knew Adrian. He made sure of it."

"That's not an answer."

Mara rubbed a thumb across the cuff of her oilskin. "Yes. He was on the heritage committee. Pain in the neck. Good on old maps. Bad with people."

The body lay face down, one arm crooked under him, the other stretched towards the island. Adrian Rusk had been a tall man, made awkward by death and water. His grey hair was plastered over one ear. A dark bruise showed at the left temple where the skin had split against bone or stone. His coat had ridden up, exposing the belt of his cord trousers. One brown shoe remained on his foot. The other was gone.

Rowan crouched at the edge of the scene and let herself look slowly. That was the first discipline: do not decide too soon.

There was weed snagged at his sleeve, as expected. Sand on the cuffs, as expected. A smear of dark grit on the back of one hand, not quite the same as the pale beach sand around him. A line of waxy residue on the collar of his coat, as if something polished had rubbed there. His right trouser pocket had been turned partly inside out.

"Paramedics?"

"Declared at six twenty-eight," Kit said. "No resuscitation attempted. He'd been in the water for hours."

"Any ID?"

"Wallet still in inside pocket. Cash untouched. Phone in waterproof pouch, dead from impact or water ingress. No notebook."

Rowan looked up. "Why mention a notebook?"

Mara answered before Kit could. "Because Adrian always had one. Little black thing, elastic round it. Wrote down everything you said, especially if you wished he wouldn't."

The gulls rose as a group from the fish market roof, white bodies flicking in the wind. Out on the island, Cormorant House showed its broken windows to the shore. The old quarantine building had been empty for most of Rowan's life. In childhood it had seemed romantic. Later she learned it was merely damp, unsafe, and expensive to save, which explained why Theo Sparke now wanted to wrap it in glass and sell it as heritage apartments to people who did not know what a winter tide could do.

"Who last saw Rusk alive?" Rowan asked.

"He was at the Lookout Arms about half seven," Mara said. "Arguing."

"With whom?"

"Depends who you ask. Theo Sparke, Nessa Quill, half the bar by the end. He had a talent."

"About the development?"

Mara's mouth tightened. "About the Mercy Gale."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. Even Kit, who was not local, had heard of it by then. Every town had its old disaster, polished by retelling until it became less an event than a moral possession. Pelham Hard had the Mercy Gale: November 1998, lifeboat out in impossible weather, two men dead, a third disgraced, the Sparke family praised for raising money afterwards until their name was welded to courage.

Rowan's father had been on that boat. He had lived. He had said almost nothing about it in the twenty-eight years since.

"What was Rusk saying about it?" she asked.

"That everyone had got it wrong."

"Did he say how?"

"He said he had proof."

Rowan stood. The wind carried the smell of diesel from the harbour and something sweeter underneath, the rotten vegetable breath of the flats. The tide had drawn back now far enough to expose the lower stones. Water still moved through the gaps, quick and glittering.

"The tide board says low water was twenty-one forty," she said.

Mara looked at the sign, then away. "If it says that, it's wrong."

"How wrong?"

"Twenty-seven minutes. Give or take."

"Enough to strand a man who thought he had time."

"Adrian wasn't a tourist."

"No," Rowan said. "He was a historian with a missing notebook, a head injury, and an argument in the pub."

Mara's eyes shifted back to the body.

Kit said, "Accident until proven otherwise?"

Rowan watched one of the cormorants lift from the island roof and skim low over the causeway. It passed over Adrian Rusk without turning its head.

"No," she said. "The other way round."
