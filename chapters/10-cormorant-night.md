# Chapter 10: Cormorant Night

Night made St Osyth's Eye less picturesque and more honest.

The island was a black shape beyond the harbour lights, low against a sky without stars. The causeway showed in fragments under Rowan's torch, each wet stone slick as bone. Wind moved across the flats with nothing to slow it. Somewhere in the dark, cormorants shifted and made low, irritated sounds in their sleep.

Mara checked her watch. "We have thirty-five minutes on the island. No heroics."

"I dislike how often people say that to me."

"Then stop looking like you need it said."

Kit, behind them, said, "This friendship has texture."

"This friendship has evidence of previous offending," Mara said, and stepped onto the stones.

They crossed without torches for the first half, using the harbour glow and Mara's sense of the path. Two uniformed officers waited on the beach. Another pair watched the kiln slip from the sail lofts. If Eleanor came, Rowan wanted her seen. If she did not, Rowan wanted the island searched before weather took the chance.

They settled behind the broken wall of the old boathouse. Cold came up through Rowan's knees. Kit handed her coffee from a flask, which tasted of metal and poor choices. Mara stood listening.

At 22:54 a pale light moved on the water near the kiln beach.

Not a torch. Too low. A shaded lantern on a small boat.

Mara leaned close. "Tender. Electric motor."

The light went out. A minute later, wood nudged stone below the boathouse. Footsteps scraped on the slip.

Eleanor Sparke came into view wearing a dark coat and boots, one hand against the wall, the other holding a small torch with a red filter. No stick tonight. No pearls. Without her public arrangement, she looked older and more dangerous, not because age had given her force, but because it had taken away her interest in appearing kind.

She went directly to the landward side of the boathouse and knelt by a stack of fallen bricks.

Rowan waited until Eleanor had lifted the third brick and drawn out an oilskin packet.

"Mrs Sparke."

Eleanor froze. The torch beam held steady on the wall.

"Put the packet down," Rowan said.

For a moment, Rowan thought Eleanor might run. Instead, she stood very slowly and turned.

"Inspector Bell," she said. "You are persistent."

"It's in the job description."

Kit stepped out to Eleanor's right. Mara moved behind her, cutting off the slip.

Eleanor looked from one to the other. "You have made quite a production."

"Open the packet," Rowan said.

"I would rather wait for my solicitor."

"Then I will open it."

Eleanor held it out. Her gloved hand did not shake.

Inside was Adrian Rusk's black notebook, swollen by damp but legible, the missing harbour log page in a plastic sleeve, Eddie Lanyon's brass whistle, and a silver cigarette case. Rowan recognised the case before she opened it from Max's description: engraved initials, tarnished hinge, mother-of-pearl inlay chipped from one corner. Inside, clipped into a narrow slot, was a folding knife with a curved blade.

The knife had been cleaned. The groove near the hinge still held dark fibres.

Kit photographed everything.

Eleanor watched him. "You don't understand what that is."

"A knife used to cut a towline in 1998," Rowan said. "And evidence Adrian found before he died."

"You say used. You cannot know."

"Adrian knew."

The name touched something. Eleanor's mouth compressed.

"Adrian knew very little about mercy," she said.

The tide moved below them, a dark soft rush against stone. Mara looked towards the causeway.

"Five minutes," she said.

Eleanor smiled. "You see? The island dislikes conversation."

"Then we'll continue on the beach," Rowan said.

They started back with Eleanor between Rowan and Kit. The crossing had already changed. Water laced the lower stones. The gaps were louder. Halfway across, Eleanor stopped.

"Mrs Sparke," Kit said. "Keep moving."

She looked towards Pelham Hard. The harbour lights trembled on the water. For the first time that night, the polish left her voice.

"Do you know what that town would have done with the truth in 1998? Nothing useful. It would have devoured itself. Eddie was dead. Jack was dead. Christopher was destroyed in every way that mattered to me. What good would it have done?"

"Tell that to Eddie's mother," Rowan said.

Eleanor turned on her. "Do not presume to instruct me on grief."

The words cracked across the water.

Then her boot slipped.

Rowan caught her by the sleeve before she went sideways into the channel. Eleanor's weight pulled hard against her shoulder. Kit grabbed Rowan's belt. Mara swore, planted herself lower on the stones, and took Eleanor's other arm.

For three ugly seconds, all four of them were only bodies against water.

They got her upright. Eleanor's face was grey.

"Move," Mara said, and this time no one answered.

On the beach, uniform took Eleanor to a waiting car. She did not resist. She asked for her solicitor, her medication, and a blanket, in that order.

Rowan stood by the warning board, breath hurting in her chest, and watched the tide cover the first stones of the causeway.

Kit came beside her. "You all right?"

"Yes."

"That was an unconvincing pause."

"I'm all right."

He held up the evidence bag containing Adrian's notebook. "This may be our cleanest voice from beyond the grave."

"Adrian would enjoy that phrasing."

"I feared as much."

Mara joined them, wet to the knee and furious. "Next time you want to use my causeway as a courtroom, don't."

"Noted."

"No, not noted. Understood."

Rowan looked at her old friend in the harbour light. "Understood."

The tide moved over the false number on the beach board's lower edge, black and shining. By morning, the causeway would look innocent again. That was the trick of tides. They erased the scene without altering what had happened.
