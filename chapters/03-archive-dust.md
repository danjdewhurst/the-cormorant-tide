# Chapter 3: Archive Dust

Pelham Hard Museum had a smugglers' lantern in the front window and a handwritten sign apologising for irregular opening hours. Rowan had always thought both were honest advertising.

Inside, the ground floor was arranged for school parties and wet Tuesdays: model trawlers in glass cases, a stuffed gull with one furious yellow eye, sepia photographs of men in ganseys, and a display about the Mercy Gale set under a lifeboat-orange board. Christopher Sparke's portrait occupied the centre. He stood in evening dress at a fundraising dinner, one hand on a silver cup, handsome in the smooth way of men used to being thanked.

Nessa Quill met them at the foot of the narrow stairs.

"If this is about Adrian's papers, I already boxed what he left here," she said.

She was thirty at most, with cropped fair hair, ink on two fingers, and the pale indoor look of someone who spent her best hours with dead people's handwriting. Grief had not reached her cleanly. Her eyes were red, but her voice had sharpened itself against use.

"You were working with him," Rowan said.

"He was working near me. Adrian did not work with people. He harvested them."

"You argued in the pub."

"Everyone argued in the pub. Pelham Hard would lose half its economy without disagreement."

The archive occupied two rooms above the public displays. The first held map drawers and a table under a skylight. The second was no wider than a corridor, lined with shelves and archive boxes. The air tasted of paper, dust, and the faint mineral damp of old stone.

Nessa put a cardboard box on the table. "This was Adrian's current material. Photocopies, transcripts, notes on the Mercy Gale, planning objections, and one truly unhinged letter to the council about interpretive signage."

Kit lifted the top folder. "No black notebook?"

"No. He had it with him last night."

"And the page he waved around?"

Nessa's face changed too quickly. It became blank, which was an answer in itself.

Rowan pulled out a chair. "Sit down."

"I prefer standing."

"I prefer honesty, but we're all making compromises."

For a moment Nessa looked as though she might tell Rowan to get out. Then she sat.

"It was a harbour log page," she said. "Or part of one. November 1998. The ink was damaged, but there were call signs and times. Adrian thought it proved the Sparke launch came in before the lifeboat was damaged, not after."

Kit glanced at Rowan. "Why does that matter?"

Nessa pressed both palms flat on the table. "The official account says Christopher Sparke tried to help after the lifeboat was already in trouble. If his launch was in the channel before the collision, then he was not a rescuer. He was part of the incident."

Rowan let the phrase sit. Part of the incident. A museum phrase, careful and bloodless.

"Where did Adrian get the page?"

"He wouldn't say."

"But you know."

"I suspect."

"Say the suspicion."

Nessa looked towards the second room. "Lily Tye brought in a rusted biscuit tin two weeks ago. She's one of our volunteers. She mudlarks under the old sail lofts, which I have told her not to do because the wall is rotten and the council will blame me when it collapses. The tin had buttons, clay pipe stems, a brass tag from the old lifeboat shed, and scraps of paper stuck together with mud."

"Including the log page."

"I did not realise at first. Adrian did. He came in while I was trying to separate them. He became extremely still, which with Adrian was as close as he got to religious experience."

"Where's the tin now?"

"Locked in the conservation cupboard."

Nessa went to fetch it. Kit watched her cross the room. "She is either frightened or very angry."

"Both can be true."

The tin smelled of iron and creek mud. Inside, each object sat in a labelled tray. The brass tag read PHLB 3. One clay pipe stem. Two blackened buttons. A twist of green glass. A strip of paper under a transparent sleeve, its writing mostly dissolved.

Rowan leaned over it.

Only fragments remained legible:

21:02 OSPR...

... no light shown

LANYON aboard

... tow cut by C.S.

Kit read silently, then said, "C.S. could be Christopher Sparke."

"It could be Coast Station," Nessa said.

"You don't sound convinced."

"I'm paid to be precise, not comforting."

Rowan looked at the public display downstairs through the stairwell gap. Christopher Sparke smiled from his orange board.

"Who knew Lily found this?"

"Adrian. Me. Lily, obviously. I told the chair of trustees there might be a significant Mercy Gale item requiring conservation funding."

"Who is chair?"

Nessa's expression answered before she did. "Eleanor Sparke."

Kit exhaled softly.

At the far end of the archive, a floorboard creaked. Rowan turned.

A girl stood half in the doorway, rain-spotted parka zipped to the chin, phone gripped in one hand. Seventeen, maybe. Dark curls escaping from a wool hat. She looked at the tin on the table and went white.

"Lily?" Rowan said.

The girl backed away.

Nessa stood. "Lily, wait."

But Lily Tye had already turned. Her trainers hammered down the stairs, past the smugglers' lantern, out into the street. The front bell gave a frantic little jangle.

Kit moved towards the stairs.

Rowan stopped him. "No. Let her get as far as the corner."

"Why?"

"Because if she's running from us, we can find her. If she's running from someone else, I want to know who watches her go."

They reached the front window in time to see Lily cross the road towards the quay. On the opposite pavement, under the striped awning of the closed ice-cream shop, Theo Sparke stood with a takeaway coffee in one hand and his phone in the other.

He watched Lily run.

Then he looked up at the museum window and saw Rowan looking back.

Theo smiled as if caught doing something charming. It was a good smile, expensive and practised. It did not reach his eyes.
