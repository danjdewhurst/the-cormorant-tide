# Chapter 11: The Matriarch

Eleanor Sparke gave no comment for fifty-eight minutes.

She sat in Interview Room Two with a blanket around her shoulders, solicitor beside her, and a paper cup of tea cooling untouched near her hand. Without the house, the pearls, the view, and the correct chair, she seemed neither diminished nor exposed. She seemed concentrated.

Rowan placed each item on the table in its evidence bag.

The tide-board key. The glove fibres. The walking stick. The mother-of-pearl fragment. Lily's head torch bearing Adrian's blood and Eleanor's print. The notebook. The harbour log page. Eddie Lanyon's whistle. The cigarette case and knife.

Eleanor watched the procession as if judging table settings.

"For the recording," Rowan said, "I am showing Mrs Sparke item RS slash seventeen, a black notebook recovered from St Osyth's Eye on Thursday night."

"No comment," Eleanor said.

"Page thirty-two. Adrian Rusk wrote, 'E.S. wants private meeting. Says she has Christopher's diary. Bring log page. Cormorant House, after pub. Tide board says time enough, but check.'"

The solicitor leaned towards Eleanor. Eleanor did not move.

"He did check," Rowan said. "At least he tried. The harbour tide board had been altered. We recovered your key."

"No comment."

"He went first to the sail lofts, then by boat from the kiln beach. You met him at Cormorant House. He had Lily Tye's head torch. You brought the cigarette case because he had asked about the knife."

Eleanor looked at the bagged silver case then. Only briefly.

"He had already found it, hadn't he?" Rowan said. "In the old hiding place. Silas Reed hid the whistle and draft statement in town, but someone else hid the knife on the island. Christopher?"

At the name, Eleanor's expression shifted.

"Christopher was many things," she said.

Her solicitor touched her arm. "Eleanor."

She withdrew from the touch. "He was vain, foolish with money, and terrified of being ordinary. He was not evil."

Rowan said nothing.

Eleanor looked at the silver case. "The Osprey should never have been out that night. I said so before dinner. He laughed. Men like Christopher treated weather as something that happened to other people."

"What was on the launch?"

"Salvage." She said it with distaste, not guilt. "Coins, mostly. A dealer in Rye. Christopher had debts. He thought a little private enterprise would save everyone embarrassment."

"Then the engine failed."

"Yes."

"The lifeboat came."

Eleanor's eyes were not in the room now. "They were magnificent. That is the part people forget when they talk about lies. Those men came out in weather that made the deck vanish under them. Eddie Lanyon got the line across. He was smiling, I remember that. Smiling in that appalling rain."

For the first time, her voice faltered.

"Christopher saw the customs launch light beyond the harbour mouth. He panicked. If we were towed in, the cargo would be found. The debts, the salvage, the insurance, all of it. He cut the tow. He said he would reconnect it after we drifted clear."

"With your knife."

"It was in my case. He took it. I did not stop him."

"The Osprey hit the lifeboat."

"Yes."

"Eddie was blamed."

Eleanor's face hardened again, the old muscles returning. "Eddie was dead. Christopher was alive. Theo was twelve. The lifeboat fund needed money. Jack Prowse's widow needed money. Do you imagine truth pays school fees and mortgages? Do you imagine a town survives on purity?"

"Eddie's mother survived on a lie."

"Everyone survives on something."

Rowan let the silence lengthen. Eleanor had begun. People who began often hated stopping.

"Adrian found the log page," Rowan said. "He found the knife. He asked you to meet because he wanted you to confirm it."

"He wanted me to kneel," Eleanor said. "He wanted a performance of remorse he could quote."

"So you struck him."

"He said Christopher murdered those men. He said I had murdered Eddie a second time. He waved that wretched whistle at me." Her hand closed around nothing on the table. "I wanted him quiet."

"You hit him with the walking stick."

"Once."

"Then you moved him to the causeway."

Eleanor looked up. "He was breathing."

"Yes."

"I thought someone would find him before the water."

It was the first shabby lie she had told badly. Even her solicitor looked away.

Rowan said, "You altered the tide board before you met him. You created an accident and then used it."

Eleanor's mouth tightened.

"Why hide the notebook on the island?" Kit asked from the corner.

She seemed almost offended by the practicality of the question. "The tide came faster than I expected. Adrian was heavier than he looked. I had his things, the torch, the case, the whistle. I could not carry everything back and manage the boat. I meant to return before daylight."

"But the island was sealed."

"Yes."

"And when you saw us find the mother-of-pearl, you knew we were close."

Eleanor looked at Rowan. "You held it up like a school prize."

"It worked."

For a second, something like admiration passed across Eleanor's face, or perhaps contempt wearing the same coat.

"Do you know what your father said to me after the inquest?" she asked.

Rowan did not answer.

"He said, 'Let the dead rest.' Sensible man."

Rowan thought of Max in his chair, of guilt turning memory into weeded bits, of Eddie Lanyon smiling in the rain because he had not yet learned what the living would do to him.

"No," she said. "He was tired."

Eleanor looked down at the cooling tea.

The interview ended at 02:14. Eleanor Sparke was charged with the murder of Adrian Rusk before dawn, with further files opened on perverting the course of justice in relation to the Mercy Gale. Outside the station, morning spread thinly over Pelham Hard. The harbour looked washed and ordinary. Men unloaded fish. A delivery van reversed badly. The bakery lights went on.

The town had not yet understood that its favourite story had died in the night.

Rowan stood on the pavement with Kit, both of them too tired to speak for a while.

At last Kit said, "She almost made it sound reasonable."

"That was her talent."

"And yours?"

Rowan looked towards the harbour, where the tide board was being corrected under uniform supervision.

"Reading numbers."
