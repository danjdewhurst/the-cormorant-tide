# Chapter 2: The Tide Board

By nine o'clock, Pelham Hard had done what Pelham Hard did best. It had made itself small and busy.

The fishmonger opened late and blamed the police tape. The bakery put a tray of buns in the window as if sugar might steady the place. Outside the chandlery, three retired men in identical flat caps stood with their backs to the wind and claimed, in turn, that they had always known Adrian Rusk would come to trouble, that nobody could have foreseen such a thing, and that the council should have closed the causeway years ago.

Rowan took the harbour office before the rumours warmed through.

It was a square room above the stores, with a view across the quay and a smell of salt, printer ink, and old damp carpet. Tide tables were pinned to one wall. Keys hung in a locked cabinet behind the desk, each with a plastic tag written in Mara Fenwick's neat block capitals.

Mara opened the tide-board file without being asked. "Official printout from the harbour app. Yesterday's evening low: twenty-one thirteen. Safe crossing window should have ended by twenty-one thirty at the latest. On a spring tide, earlier if you're cautious."

Rowan looked at the paper. "The board on the beach said twenty-one forty."

"Yes."

"When did you set it?"

"Seven thirty yesterday morning."

"To twenty-one thirteen?"

"Yes."

"Who can change it?"

Mara took a breath through her nose. "Me. Assistant harbourmaster. Two quay wardens. Coastguard box has a spare. Lifeboat station has a spare. Museum has one for school tours, because God forbid a child should have to look through smeared glass. And the council office has a master key they think I don't know about."

"So half the town."

"The careful half."

Kit, standing by the window with his notebook, said, "Any CCTV on the board?"

"No. On the harbour mouth and fuel berth, yes. On a warning sign everyone ignores until they need someone to blame, no."

Rowan walked to the key cabinet. "Any keys missing?"

"Not now."

"That's not what I asked."

Mara's jaw moved. "The museum key was missing for three hours last week. Nessa Quill reported it herself. She found it in the archive sink, which tells you more about the state of that building than about criminal genius."

"And yesterday?"

"I didn't audit the keys yesterday."

"Why not?"

"Because I run a harbour, Rowan, not a prison."

The use of her first name was a little push. Rowan let it go. "Where were you between seven and ten last night?"

"Here until eight. Then home."

"Alone?"

"Yes."

"Can anyone confirm that?"

"My electricity meter, if it's chatty."

Kit did not look up, but Rowan felt him register the answer. Mara had always lied badly when cornered. Not because she lacked intelligence, but because contempt arrived before strategy.

Rowan turned back to the desk. Adrian Rusk's phone lay in an evidence bag beside his wallet. The phone had not died entirely. A technician had coaxed enough from it to recover the last few notifications, no content yet, only times and senders.

"Rusk received a text at eighteen fifty-two," Kit said. "Unknown number. Burner, likely. We have the first line from notification preview: 'Bring the page. Cormorants know the rest.'"

Mara frowned. "What page?"

"You tell us."

"I don't know."

"He was researching the Mercy Gale," Rowan said. "Had he found something?"

Mara folded her arms. "Adrian always found something. Mostly offence."

"You said he claimed he had proof."

"He did. At the pub. He waved a piece of paper around until Nessa told him to put it away before he got beer on it."

"What did it show?"

"I wasn't close enough."

"Mara."

"It looked like a log page," she said. "Harbour log, maybe. Old. Water-damaged."

Outside, a forklift beeped along the quay. A crate of sole went past the window, silver and flat as thrown knives.

Rowan said, "Why would a harbour log matter?"

Mara looked at the tide tables on the wall. "The night of the Mercy Gale, the harbour log went missing. Officially, it was ruined by flood water. People have been arguing about that since I was fourteen."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, it said things people didn't want said."

Kit closed his notebook. "About Christopher Sparke?"

The room altered. Not visibly. No door opened, no floorboard shifted. But Mara's face became a harbour door in bad weather: barred from inside.

"You should talk to the Sparkes yourself," she said.

"We will."

"Theo will act like a fool. Don't mistake that for depth. Eleanor's the one who remembers everything."

Rowan looked again at the key cabinet. "Does Eleanor Sparke have a tide-board key?"

"Not officially."

"And unofficially?"

Mara gave a small, humourless laugh. "Eleanor has keys to rooms that haven't been built yet."

By ten thirty, Kit had the harbour camera footage. It showed Adrian Rusk at 20:18, walking alone past the fuel berth, away from the pub and towards the derelict sail lofts. He wore the same brown coat recovered from the causeway. He carried something under his arm, flat and wrapped in newspaper. Ten minutes later the camera caught the blur of a dark car on the service road behind the lofts. The plate was unreadable because rain had smeared the lens.

"Not the route to the island," Kit said.

"No," Rowan said. "It's the route to the old kiln beach."

"Which is?"

"Where you launch if you don't want the harbour camera seeing you launch."

Kit watched the footage again. "You said that like someone who once launched there for reasons her father never heard about."

"My father heard about everything. Usually before I got home."

The joke cost more than it should have. Max Bell was in his bungalow above the allotments, probably listening to Radio 4 too loudly and pretending not to wait for her call. Rowan had not told him about Adrian yet. She was not sure whether she was protecting him from shock or herself from the questions.

At noon the pathologist rang with the preliminary view. Drowning was likely, but the injury to Adrian's temple had occurred before death. A fall could explain it. So could a blow. There were fibres caught in the split skin: dark wool, perhaps from a glove. There was no sign of robbery beyond the missing notebook and whatever paper he had carried under his arm.

Rowan wrote it down.

Then she looked at the harbour tide printout again, at the false time behind glass, at the neat lie sitting where public safety should have been.

Some murders shouted. This one had adjusted a number and let the sea speak.
