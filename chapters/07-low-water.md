# Chapter 7: Low Water

Low water came at 14:06, and with it the island revealed itself reluctantly.

The causeway emerged in patches: first the crown of the stones, then the weed-slick sides, then the channels between them where water hurried seaward in brown glass ribbons. Uniform kept the public back at the beach. A few residents gathered anyway, hoods up, pretending not to be watching a murder inquiry with the appetite of theatre.

Mara Fenwick stood beside Rowan at the warning board. "You've got forty minutes before I'd want anyone turning back."

"The table gives us fifty-five."

"The table wasn't born here."

Rowan looked at her. "You still say you were home after eight?"

"Yes."

"You know we'll check."

"I assumed policing had advanced that far."

"Mara."

The harbourmaster's face tightened. "I went out."

"Where?"

"To my father's."

"Why lie?"

"Because he was drunk, and because he told me he saw Adrian with a woman at the kiln beach, and because when I asked which woman he said, 'the one with death on her coat,' so I decided not to build your case on it."

"Your father is Patrick Fenwick."

"Yes."

"He was on shore during the Mercy Gale."

"Everyone was somewhere during the Mercy Gale. It's Pelham Hard's favourite alibi."

Rowan held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded towards the island. "You lead."

They crossed in single file: Mara first, Rowan behind her, Kit behind Rowan with a scene kit slung over one shoulder, and two search officers after him. The stones were treacherous underfoot. Weed slid and sucked beneath their boots. On either side, the exposed flats gleamed with worm casts and shallow pools. The air smelled metallic, stripped clean by the ebb.

At the island, Cormorant House rose from coarse grass and nettles, its brickwork furred with salt, its roof broken along one side. Birds shifted and muttered above the empty windows. The old boathouse leaned towards the channel as if considering surrender.

"Adrian came here in the afternoon," Rowan said. "He borrowed Lily's head torch."

"Then he was a bigger fool than I thought."

"You never came here?"

Mara gave her a look. "We all came here. It was where you went when you wanted to be older than you were."

Rowan remembered a bottle of cider, a dare, Mara's hand warm around her wrist as they ran back from the rising tide. Memory made children of them without permission.

The cellar entrance was half hidden by collapsed masonry on the seaward side. At ordinary low water it remained flooded. On a spring ebb, a narrow gap opened under the broken step, just high enough for a person willing to crawl.

Kit shone his torch inside. "Charming."

"After you," Rowan said.

"Rank has privileges."

Mara snorted and went first.

The cellar stank of brine, bird droppings, and old lime mortar. Water dripped steadily from the ceiling. Rowan's torch found rusted hooks, broken bottles, a crab moving with delicate outrage, and a recess in the wall where newer scratches marked the brick. Something had been pulled from there recently.

Kit photographed the recess. "The thing they couldn't drown?"

"Maybe."

On the floor beneath it lay a torn strip of newspaper, still damp. Rowan lifted it with tweezers. The date on the scrap was from the previous week. Adrian must have wrapped something here after finding it, or unwrapped something to carry away.

Mara, crouched by the steps, said, "Blood."

The stain was small, a brownish smear on the edge of a brick at shoulder height. Nearby, snagged on a nail, was a thread of dark wool.

"Not where he was found," Kit said.

"No."

Rowan looked back towards the causeway through the cellar opening. The killer had not struck Adrian on the exposed stones. He had been hit here or near here, where the old house kept wind and sound inside. Then moved, perhaps half conscious, perhaps already helpless, to the causeway. Left where the water would do the rest.

Something pale glinted in a crack near the wall. Rowan eased it free with the tweezers. It was a tiny crescent of mother-of-pearl, no larger than a fingernail clipping, backed by a smear of old adhesive.

Kit leaned in. "Button?"

"Inlay," Mara said.

Rowan looked at her.

"From a fancy stick or knife handle, maybe. My grandfather had a tobacco box with pieces like that."

Eleanor Sparke's silver cormorant stick had a mother-of-pearl eye.

Rowan bagged the fragment.

Outside, one of the search officers called from the grass. They had found Lily's head torch under a tangle of nettles, cracked at the lens. The battery still worked. On the elastic strap, half hidden in mud, someone had left a partial fingerprint in blood.

Kit's eyebrows lifted. "That would be helpful."

"Careful," Rowan said. "The case might hear you."

They searched until Mara's patience ran out before the tide did.

"Back," she said. "Now."

The return crossing was uglier. Water had begun nosing through the lower channels, filling spaces that had been stone ten minutes before. The causeway changed under their feet with each step. Halfway across, Rowan turned and looked back.

Cormorant House stood above the lifting water, windows black, birds hunched along the roofline. It was easy to see why Adrian had made a riddle of it. At the right tide, the island gave up secrets. At the wrong one, it kept them forever.

On the beach, a crowd waited beyond the cordon. Eleanor Sparke stood among them beneath a navy umbrella. When Rowan stepped off the causeway, Eleanor inclined her head.

Not greeting. Assessment.

Rowan held up the evidence bag just high enough for Eleanor to see the mother-of-pearl crescent.

For the first time since they had met, Eleanor Sparke forgot to arrange her face.

Only for a second.

It was enough.
