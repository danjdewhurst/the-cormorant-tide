# Chapter 8: The Net Loft

Silas Reed lived above the net loft behind the chandlery, in two rooms that smelled of tobacco, rope, and kippers. He had ignored three calls, one visit from a constable, and a note pushed through his door. At seven that evening his neighbour reported hearing a crash and "language unsuitable for any Christian wall."

Rowan found him sitting on the floor with blood down one side of his face and a broken chair beside him.

"Ambulance is coming," she said.

"Don't need one."

"Your head disagrees."

"My head's always been dramatic."

He was eighty-one, wiry, and furious at being seen vulnerable. Kit checked the rooms while Rowan pressed a towel to the cut above Silas's eyebrow. The little kitchen was tidy. The sitting room was not. Drawers had been opened, cushions thrown, framed photographs tipped face down on the carpet.

"Who did this?" Rowan asked.

"Didn't see."

"Try again."

"Door went. Thought it was you lot. Someone came up behind me. Next thing, floor."

"Man? Woman?"

"Heavy step. That's all."

"What were they looking for?"

Silas's eyes slid towards the cold fireplace.

Rowan followed the look. "Kit."

Kit crouched and felt along the soot-blackened bricks. A loose one shifted. Behind it was a cloth packet tied with string.

Silas swore with feeling.

Inside the packet lay three objects: a lifeboat crew photograph, a folded statement from 1998, and a small brass whistle greened by age.

"Adrian's missing whistle?" Kit asked.

Silas shook his head. "Eddie Lanyon's. Took it from the boat shed after they said what they said about him. His mother couldn't bear to ask for it. I meant to give it back and then it got too late, same as everything."

Rowan opened the folded statement. It was a draft, never signed. Silas Reed's name was typed at the top. The date was December 1998.

I saw Mr Christopher Sparke cut the towline between Osprey and Pelham Hard lifeboat. I saw Mrs Sparke standing beside him with a torch. Eddie Lanyon was forward at that time and could not have reached the stern cleat.

Rowan read it twice.

"Why didn't you sign?"

Silas stared at the opposite wall. "Because Christopher came to the quay with his lawyer. Said memories go funny in storms. Said if I made trouble, the insurance might not pay the lifeboat fund, and Eddie's mother would lose the hardship grant. Said people would ask why I was sober enough to remember but not brave enough to help."

"Were you?"

"Sober? No."

"Brave enough?"

His mouth worked. "No."

There it was again: not villainy alone, but a town made from small surrenders.

"Did Adrian know about this statement?"

"He guessed. Came badgering. I told him to leave dead boys alone."

"Did you give him anything?"

Silas nodded at the whistle. "He took that yesterday afternoon. Said if Eddie's name was in his talk, Eddie should have a voice in the room. Dramatic sod."

"The whistle wasn't on his body."

"Then whoever killed him took it."

Kit came back from the bedroom. "No forced entry. Back window open over the flat roof."

Silas snorted. "No one over fifty came through that."

"Theo Sparke?" Rowan asked.

"Maybe. He's got more hair oil than sense, but he's not soft."

"Eleanor?"

Silas gave her a look. "Eleanor never climbs through windows. She makes you open the door and feel grateful for the chance."

The ambulance crew arrived and began the gentle argument required to remove an unwilling old man from his own floor. Rowan stepped aside to take a call from forensics.

The blood on Lily's head torch was Adrian's. The partial fingerprint over it did not match Adrian, Lily, Rowan, Kit, Mara, Theo, or Nessa. Eleanor Sparke's elimination prints had not yet been provided. Her solicitor had asked whether an appointment next week would be convenient.

"Tell them no," Rowan said.

"Already did," the forensic officer replied.

At the hospital, Silas refused stitches until a nurse half his size told him to stop performing masculinity near her clean trolley. Rowan waited through the treatment and then took his formal statement in a curtained bay that smelled of disinfectant and vending-machine soup.

"There's more," Silas said at last.

Rowan lowered her pen.

"After the inquest, Eleanor came to see me. Not Christopher. Eleanor. She said Eddie's mother had suffered enough and didn't need my guilt dressed up as honesty. She said Pelham Hard needed a story it could live with."

"And did it?"

"Live with it?" Silas looked at his hands. "Look around."

On the drive back, Kit was quiet until the coast road dipped and the lights of Pelham Hard appeared below them.

"We have motive for Eleanor," he said. "Maybe for Theo if the development collapses. Opportunity depends on the tide-board key and the island. Physical evidence maybe points to her stick, but not enough yet."

"And someone assaulted Silas."

"Could be Theo cleaning up for his mother."

"Could be someone trying to make us think that."

Kit leaned his head back against the seat. "I miss burglaries. Burglars rarely have heritage committees."

Rowan almost smiled.

Her phone rang as they reached the harbour. The caller ID showed Max.

"Dad?"

For a moment she heard only his breathing and the faint murmur of the radio.

"I remembered something," he said.

"What?"

"The light on the Sparke boat. It wasn't a torch. It was Eleanor's cigarette case. Silver. Christopher used it to cut the line because the knife was in it."

Rowan pulled into the kerb.

"Dad, a cigarette case can't cut a towline."

"No. But it had the knife clipped inside. Fancy thing. Mother-of-pearl on the handle. She carried it everywhere in those days. Said a woman should be able to open her own parcels."

Mother-of-pearl.

Rowan looked across the harbour at Sparke House on the hill, its windows lit above the town like a ship that had never intended to answer distress.
