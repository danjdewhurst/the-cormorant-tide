# Chapter 4: The Mercy Gale

Rowan postponed her father until late afternoon, which was both practical and cowardly. She told herself she needed the archive copies first. She told herself Kit could interview Theo Sparke without her for twenty minutes. She told herself many useful things while driving up the hill to the bungalow where Max Bell had lived since her mother died.

He opened the door before she knocked.

"Police car gives you away," he said.

"It's a pool car. It gives everyone away."

Max Bell had shrunk in the ordinary, infuriating manner of ageing men who had once seemed built into the world. His shoulders were narrower. His beard had gone white. But his eyes were still the exact blue Rowan remembered from childhood, a startling colour in a weathered face.

The sitting room smelled of tea, furniture polish, and the sea because all houses in Pelham Hard eventually did. On the mantelpiece stood three photographs: Rowan at graduation, her mother laughing at something outside the frame, and the lifeboat crew in 1998, taken six months before the Mercy Gale.

Max saw her looking.

"You're here about Adrian Rusk."

"You heard."

"I may be elderly, not buried."

"Dad."

"Mara rang."

Of course she had. Rowan took the armchair opposite him. The upholstery was worn where her mother's hands had rested for years. "Adrian was researching the Mercy Gale."

Max turned his mug by the handle. "Adrian researched whatever made people shut doors."

"Did he speak to you?"

"Several times. I told him what I told the inquest."

"Which was?"

"Bad night. Bad water. Worse luck."

"That's not evidence."

"It was enough for the inquest."

Rowan felt the old irritation rise, hot as fever. Her father could be affectionate, funny, generous, brave. He could also close a subject so completely that a person might bruise herself against it for years.

"There may be a harbour log page," she said. "It suggests Christopher Sparke's launch was involved before the lifeboat took damage."

Max closed his eyes.

The movement frightened her more than denial would have.

"Did you know?" she asked.

"I knew the sea was full of boats that night."

"Dad."

"No." He opened his eyes. "I don't know what you mean by know. People think memory is a rope. Pull one end and the whole thing comes in. It isn't. It comes up in weeded bits."

"Then give me the bits."

For a while he listened to the house settling around them. A gull screamed down by the allotments. The central heating ticked in the pipes.

"We got the shout at twenty past nine," he said. "Launch in distress near the outer channel. Weather already foul. Wind south-west, too much of it. We went out because that's what you do."

"Who was on the launch?"

"Christopher Sparke. Eleanor. One of their guests, I think. Maybe two. They'd been at some dinner along the coast and thought they could beat the weather home."

"Could they?"

Max gave her a look. "No."

"What happened?"

"We reached them off St Osyth's Eye. Their engine had failed. They were drifting wrong. We got a towline across, or Eddie did. Eddie Lanyon. Nineteen, keen as paint, hands quicker than thought."

He stopped.

Rowan waited.

"The line went," he said. "Officially Eddie cut it in panic after it fouled. That was what Christopher said. Eddie was washed over before anyone could ask him. Jack Prowse went after him. We lost both."

"And unofficially?"

Max's hands tightened around the mug until Rowan thought it might crack.

"I saw a light where there shouldn't have been a light."

"On the Sparke launch?"

"A torch, maybe. Low down by the stern. Then the line jerked. Then their boat slewed towards us and hit. After that it was noise."

"Did you tell the inquest?"

"I said there was confusion."

"That isn't the same."

"No."

The word sat there, plain and unforgivable.

Rowan looked at the crew photograph. Her father in the back row. Young. Broad. Beside him a boy with a grin too large for his face: Eddie Lanyon, scapegoat by obituary.

"Why didn't you say more?"

Max put the mug down carefully. "Because Christopher Sparke had three lawyers and a wife who could cry without her face moving. Because Eddie was dead and his mother had already buried two sons. Because the lifeboat needed money. Because I was a coward. Choose whichever helps your report."

It was the first honest answer and the cruelest. Rowan had wanted a villain and been handed a man.

"Adrian found proof," she said.

"Then he was in danger."

"From whom?"

Max's laugh was dry and brief. "You still think danger is a person with a knife. Sometimes it's a room full of respectable people who all need the same lie."

On her way out, he called her back.

"Ro."

She turned at the door.

"Eleanor came here last week."

Rowan went still. "Why?"

"To ask if Adrian had been troubling me."

"What did you say?"

"I told her he had come round with his notebook and his clever questions."

"Did you tell her what you remembered?"

Max looked past her, through the hall window, towards the sea he could no longer see from the chair. "I told her I was tired."

It might have been true. It might also have been the only lie left to him.

Back at the station room they had borrowed from the council, Kit had pinned photographs to a whiteboard: Adrian on the causeway, the tide board, Theo outside the museum, the harbour log scrap, the route from pub to sail lofts.

"Theo Sparke says he argued with Adrian about defamatory nonsense," Kit said. "Then he left the pub at eight, walked home, made three calls to investors, and did not murder anyone because murder is bad for planning optics."

"Did he phrase it like that?"

"Almost exactly. I admired the economy."

Rowan added Eleanor Sparke's name under Theo's and drew a line to the harbour log.

Kit watched her. "Your father?"

"He saw enough in 1998 to know the official account was wrong."

"Can we use him?"

The question was procedural. It still landed like a shove.

"If we need to," she said.

Outside, the evening tide began to turn. The harbour ropes tightened one by one, each boat answering the pull before any person noticed.
